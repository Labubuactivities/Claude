# Progress Example — Worked Sample Entries

Two example session entries showing what populated `progress.md` entries look like in practice. The student is hypothetical: working professional with prior trading background, learning investing. Use these as reference for the level of specificity that makes progress logs useful vs. decorative.

-----

## Session 4 — 2026-03-12 — Investing

**Mode arc:** drill (10m) → Socratic (5m) → lecture-block on moat sources (12m) → case study: See's Candies (25m) → debate on whether moats are identifiable ex ante (15m) → Socratic close (8m)
**Stage:** 1 → moving into 2
**Duration:** ~75m

### Retrieval performance (opening drill)

- **Graham's quantitative cheapness framework (session 2):** Fully retrieved. Could state the working capital screen, the P/E hurdle, the 30%-margin-of-safety rule. Good.
- **The Buffett-vs-Graham divergence (session 3):** Partial. Got the qualitative shift but couldn't name the Phil Fisher influence or articulate why Munger mattered for the transition. Re-quiz next session.
- **Five axes of disagreement on the council (session 1):** Mostly retrieved (concentration/diversification, time horizon, qualitative/quantitative, market efficiency). Forgot leverage as the fifth axis. Note: this is the second axis-related lapse, watch for pattern.

### Today's content

**Main concept/topic:** Moat doctrine — what it is, the four canonical sources, how it differs from Graham-style cheapness.

**Council positions engaged:**

- Buffett: moats are identifiable ex ante through qualitative analysis; the four sources in the 1996 letter (scale economies, network effects, intangible assets/brand, switching costs).
- Munger: same framework, but more willing to admit moats erode silently; the latticework adds psychology-of-misjudgment to the qualitative read.
- Taleb: moats are mostly survivorship bias; the "durable advantage" story is visible only because the failures are invisible.
- Fama (implicit): persistent alpha is rare and probably factor-driven; the moat framework is a post-hoc rationalization of returns that are statistically explainable.

**Where the council split:** Buffett/Munger vs Taleb on whether moats are identifiable ex ante. Buffett/Munger vs Fama on whether the framework has predictive power at all, or just descriptive elegance.

**Cases used:**

- **See's Candies (foundational, Type A):** Buffett's 1972 purchase. Taught the shift from quantitative cheapness (it was not statistically cheap at $25M) to qualitative quality (brand, pricing power, capital-light economics). The "would I be happy if I had to hold this for 10 years with the stock market closed" test.
- **Kraft Heinz (counterexample, Type C, brief):** Same framework applied, very different outcome. Surfaced for next session as a fuller case.

**Cross-domain bridges:** Briefly invoked network effects from `05-cross-domain-synthesis.md` — Metcalfe's law as the mathematical backbone of the "demand-side moat" Buffett identified informally in the 1996 letter. Student had not previously connected these. Worth returning to.

### The student's positions

**Where the student converged with the canon:** Agreed that See's worked because of pricing power that wasn't statistically visible at purchase. Agreed that the moat framework genuinely added something Graham didn't have.

**Where the student diverged (and on what reasoning):** Pushed hard on Taleb's survivorship argument. His position: "If the framework is real, we should see Buffett-style investors consistently outperforming. We mostly don't — most active managers underperform. So either the framework is unreal, or only Buffett can apply it, which means it's not transferable knowledge." Strong argument; needed real response.

**Bluffs caught:** When asked what "switching costs" specifically meant for a software business, student gave a fluent but vague answer about "customers being locked in." Pressed for specifics, he couldn't give a real switching-cost calculation for any specific case. Surfaced this directly: "You have the term but not the concept. Let's slow down." We did, briefly.

**Debate moments:** Student defended Taleb's position better than I initially attacked it. I had to escalate the steel-manning: pointed to the existence of consistent multi-decade outperformers (Buffett, Klarman, Renaissance) as evidence that *some* extractable edge exists, and pushed him to explain that within a survivorship frame. He conceded the framework probably has some predictive power but not as much as Buffett's reputation implies — a position I actually find defensible. We agreed to leave it there as an unresolved live question.

### Confidence calibration

**Claims the student made with Level 1 confidence that should have been Level 2-3:**

- "Moats are identifiable ex ante" — he stated this flat. Pushed him to mark it Level 2 ("probably true but contested by Taleb-Fama school, would update if the empirical outperformance attributable to ex ante moat identification is weaker than I think").

**Claims they hedged appropriately:** When discussing whether the Kraft Heinz failure invalidates the framework, he hedged appropriately ("the framework is right but the application can be wrong; I don't have enough info to say which it was here"). Good.

**Calibration trajectory:** Improving. Session 2, he stated everything Level 1; session 4, he can hold Level 2 on contested questions when prompted. Still doesn't volunteer the level without prompting. Push for unprompted next session.

### Misconceptions surfaced

- "Switching costs" used as a vocabulary item without concept underneath (see above).
- Conflated "brand moat" with "marketing budget" briefly. Corrected: brand moat means customers will pay a price premium driven by trust/familiarity that survives the absence of advertising. Coca-Cola's brand is a moat; new luxury launches with heavy ad spend often aren't (yet) moats.

### Open questions / unresolved

- The Taleb survivorship vs. Buffett-school question. Genuinely unresolved between us. Carry forward; revisit after we cover Renaissance and the factor-zoo literature.
- The Kraft Heinz case: deferred to next session as a full Type C counterexample case.
- How network-effect moats specifically differ from brand moats in dynamics. The student is starting to think about this — has trading background, knows about feedback loops — but doesn't have the investing-side vocabulary yet.

### Assignment for next session

- **Reading:** Buffett 1996 letter (full); Buffett 1989 letter on the See's purchase retrospective; pp. 6-12 of the 2014 50-year retrospective (the moat discussion).
- **Thinking:** Before next session, pick a publicly traded company you think has a moat and write out: which of the four sources, what's the specific mechanism, what would erode it, how would you know ex ante you were right. ~30 min thinking.
- **Doing:** Decision journal entry on a recent trading decision he flagged in session 2. We will use this as a case in session 5.
- **Re-retrieval queue:** moat sources (3d), Phil Fisher influence (3d, since it's been missed twice), Taleb survivorship argument (1 week), See's Candies details (1 week, 1 month).

### Teacher's notes to self

- He's stronger on adversarial questions than on construction. The debate moment was where he came alive — much sharper than during the lecture-block. Lean more on debate mode for this student earlier in the curriculum than the default sequence suggests.
- Has a clear pattern of forgetting axes/lists. Two lapses now on multi-item structures (axes in session 1, four moat sources started to slip when pressed). Suggests pure-recall is weak; he stores things in narrative-causal form. Reframe lists into causal stories where possible.
- Trading background is giving him good intuitions for feedback loops and reflexivity but bad intuitions for time horizon — keeps wanting to know "by when" something will happen. Next session: explicit lecture-block on why the time-horizon question is different in investing vs. trading. Connects directly to `domains/trading.md` cross-domain note.

### Quality of the session

Strong session. Produced North Star outputs #1 (structured understanding of moat doctrine grounded in specific letters) and #3 (the switching-costs bluff was caught and is now a known weak spot). The Taleb-vs-Buffett debate produced #2 (he formulated a question — "what's the actual empirical edge from ex ante moat identification, separate from Buffett's specific record?" — that he couldn't have formulated last week). Assignment is concrete. Score: high.

## Session 5 — 2026-03-15 — Investing

**Mode arc:** drill (15m, longer than usual — covering re-retrieval queue) → case study: Kraft Heinz (35m) → Socratic on moat erosion mechanics (20m) → debate on his personal-portfolio moat pick (10m)
**Stage:** 2
**Duration:** ~80m

### Retrieval performance (opening drill)

- **Four moat sources (session 4):** Fully retrieved this time. Named all four with one-sentence mechanism for each. Good — the reframing into causal stories appears to be working.
- **Phil Fisher influence (re-quizzed, was missing in session 4):** Retrieved. Could name the *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* reference and the qualitative-quality contribution. Solid now.
- **Taleb survivorship argument (1 week interval, first re-quiz):** Partial. Got the structure but couldn't articulate the empirical anchor. We worked it back.
- **Axes of council disagreement (session 1, ~3 weeks now):** Strong on four; missed leverage again. Third miss on this specific item. Flagging this as a known weak spot — third strike.

### Today's content

**Main concept/topic:** How moats erode. Kraft Heinz as the counterexample case. The 3G Capital cost-cutting playbook and its interaction with brand-equity decay.

**Council positions engaged:**

- Buffett (post-2019): Public partial concession that the Kraft Heinz thesis broke. He'd overpaid; brand erosion under cost-cutting was real.
- Munger (post-2019): Stronger admission than Buffett, including in the 2020 annual meeting Q&A.
- 3G Capital's implicit position: brands are essentially balance-sheet items; aggressive cost discipline preserves their value as long as you don't cut the product itself.
- Industry analysts who flagged the thesis early: noted that the millennial shift in food consumption (toward fresh, away from packaged) was a structural headwind that pre-dated and survived the 3G cost-cutting.

**Where the council split:** Whether the Kraft Heinz failure was (a) framework failure (moats aren't reliably identifiable ex ante after all), (b) application failure (Buffett misjudged this specific moat), or (c) execution failure (3G's cost-cutting damaged the brand in ways unrelated to the framework). All three have defenders.

**Cases used:**

- **Kraft Heinz (counterexample, Type C):** Worked in full. Buffett 2013 entry, 2015 Kraft merger, 2019 write-down, 2020 retrospective. Compared to See's at every stage.
- **His personal-portfolio pick (the assignment):** He'd picked Costco. Defended it briefly. We agreed to do a full case next session — Costco merits its own session because of the unusual moat structure (members-pay-to-shop economics).

### The student's positions

**Where the student converged with the canon:** Agreed that the Kraft Heinz failure is genuinely interpretation-dependent — all three readings (framework / application / execution) have merit. Agreed the right discipline is humility about ex ante moat confidence.

**Where the student diverged:** Argued for reading (a) — framework failure — more strongly than I think the evidence supports. His view: "If three Berkshire-level minds with all the relevant information misjudged this, the framework is less reliable than its advertisers claim." Reasonable; pressed him to compare to medical diagnosis (also imperfect but still has predictive value). Productive exchange.

**Bluffs caught:** None this session. He came in better-prepared and didn't reach for vocabulary he didn't have.

**Debate moments:** Brief debate on Costco. He defended the moat well — specifically the membership-fee-as-customer-commitment-device mechanism. I attacked from the e-commerce angle (Amazon's threat). He responded credibly. Good defense; we left it to develop next session.

### Confidence calibration

He volunteered confidence levels twice without prompting this session. First time. Good signal.

- "Costco has a durable moat" — he volunteered as Level 2, would update if "Amazon's grocery push shows it can match Costco's bulk economics for the cost-conscious-but-loyal segment." Sharp falsification condition.
- "Kraft Heinz was primarily a framework failure" — he held Level 2.5 (his own terminology — between 2 and 3). I pushed back; he stuck with it. Reasonable.

**Calibration trajectory:** Continuing to improve. Now volunteering levels and giving real falsification conditions. Next push: get him calibrated on multi-axis claims (where one part is Level 1 and another is Level 3).

### Misconceptions surfaced

- Brief confusion between "moat erosion" (gradual, structural) and "moat failure" (the moat was never there to begin with). Corrected: these have different implications for the framework. Erosion is consistent with the framework; non-existent moats falsely identified ex ante are a framework problem.

### Open questions / unresolved

- The Costco case for next session.
- His tendency to read framework failures generously to the framework's critics, application failures generously to the framework. Watch for symmetry in interpretation next time.
- Leverage as the fifth axis of disagreement. Three strikes. Build a dedicated drill into next opening.

### Assignment for next session

- **Reading:** Costco shareholder letters last three years (~30 min); Jim Sinegal interviews specifically on the membership model.
- **Thinking:** What is the strongest reason Costco's moat could erode? Write out three specific scenarios. Don't pick the obvious "Amazon" answer.
- **Doing:** Decision journal entry on the recent trading decision (rolled forward from session 4 because we haven't done it).
- **Re-retrieval queue:** Kraft Heinz three-reading framework (3d), moat erosion vs. moat failure distinction (3d), Costco moat mechanism (1 week), leverage axis (next session, drill heavily).

### Teacher's notes to self

- He's now visibly enjoying the debate exchanges. The intensity that initially seemed to bother him is now welcome. Good — we can run debate mode more often.
- He continues to map everything to trading intuitions. This is becoming productive rather than reductive — he's starting to see the structural patterns. Cross-domain synthesis material from `references/05` is landing.
- The "trade for what time horizon" question still surfaces. Need to do that lecture-block in session 6 or 7.

### Quality of the session

Strong session. North Star #1 (clear structured understanding of why and how moats erode, grounded in Kraft Heinz specifics) and #2 (the framework/application/execution distinction is a new question for him). Re-retrieval revealed the leverage-axis weakness clearly, which is data even if the lapse itself isn't a win. Assignment is concrete and connects to next session.
