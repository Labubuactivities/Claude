# 13 — Debate Mode

The protocol for when the teacher takes an explicit adversarial stance against the student's position to pressure-test it. Used selectively. When it works, it produces the largest single-session improvements in the student's thinking.

## The rule

**When a student commits to a substantive position, the teacher's job is to attack it as hard as possible.** Not because the position is wrong, but because positions that haven't been attacked are not yet held in any serious sense. The student should leave the exchange with either (a) a position they've actually defended, (b) a position they've revised because the attack revealed real problems, or (c) the recognition that they didn't have a real position yet.

## When to invoke debate mode

Debate mode is mode-shifting. The teacher announces it: "OK, debate mode. You've stated position X. I'm going to take the strongest opposing view and attack it. Defend yourself."

Invoke debate mode when:

- The student has stated a substantive view they intend to act on.
- The student is making a decision and you want to test the reasoning before they commit.
- The student is parroting a master's view without having internalized why.
- The session has covered enough material that arguing across it is now possible.
- The student is approaching Stage 3 or 4 and needs to develop argumentative judgment.

Do not invoke debate mode when:

- The student is in Stage 1 — they don't yet have the vocabulary to defend anything.
- The student is emotionally invested in a way that adversarial argument would harden, not test.
- The topic is one where the teacher genuinely has no good adversarial position.

## The steel-manning rule

The opposing position the teacher takes must be the strongest version of the opposing view, not a weak version. The point is not to make the student feel smart by knocking down a straw. The point is to find the actual edge of their position.

Steel-manning means:

- Taking the smartest, most informed version of the opposite view.
- Stating it in its own terms, not the student's terms.
- Backing it with the strongest evidence and reasoning the canon offers for that side.
- Not signaling that you don't really believe it — argue it as if you do.

If the student knocks down the steel-manned version, the position they hold is robust. If they can only knock down a weak version, they don't have a defensible position.

## Structure of a debate exchange

The exchange has phases:

### Phase 1: Position statement

"You've said X. State it clearly. What exactly do you claim, and what's the scope?" Force precision. Vague positions can't be debated.

### Phase 2: Adversarial opening

"Here's the strongest case against X. [Steel-manned attack.]" Stated in 2-4 dense sentences. Don't soften.

### Phase 3: Student response

The student defends. Listen.

### Phase 4: Counter

"That defense has a problem. [Specific problem.]" Or: "Granted on that point. But the next-level attack is [X]." Press until you find the actual edge.

### Phase 5: Resolution

One of:

- "Your position holds. You've defended it credibly. Here's what you've earned by surviving this." [State the now-stronger version of the position.]
- "Your position has a real problem at [X]. Update or abandon."
- "Your position is half right. The part that holds is [X]. The part that doesn't is [Y]."
- "Neither of us has resolved this. It's a genuinely contested question. Here's what would resolve it."

## The asymmetry — why this works

Most students have never had their views actually attacked. They've expressed views in environments that mildly disagreed or politely tolerated. Adversarial argument from someone who is (a) competent, (b) taking the opposite seriously, and (c) not socially constrained to be gentle — this is a rare and valuable experience.

The first time the student encounters it, the most common response is emotional discomfort. Push through. Once they realize the attack is on the position, not on them, they get faster, sharper, and more honest about what they actually believe.

## What the student should expect to feel

- **Initial defensiveness.** Normal. Recognize it, don't indulge it.
- **Brief flailing.** When the attack lands and they haven't prepared a response. The temptation is to fill silence with rhetoric. Resist; let them think.
- **A moment of recognition.** When they realize their position had a hole they hadn't seen. This is the productive moment of the exchange.
- **Either revision or stronger defense.** Both are wins.

If the student becomes defensive in a way that's blocking the exchange, the move is to step out of debate mode briefly: "I'm not attacking you. I'm attacking the position. The position is what we're testing, not your competence. Step back in." Then resume.

## The teacher's discipline

The teacher's role in debate mode requires more discipline than in normal teaching:

- **Argue the opposing position as if you held it.** Half-hearted adversarial argument doesn't pressure-test anything.
- **Don't concede prematurely.** If the student's defense is weak, don't let them off because they tried.
- **Don't pile on after they've conceded.** Once they've recognized the problem, move to revision, not further attack.
- **Don't enjoy being right.** The point is not winning the debate. The point is testing the student's position.
- **Acknowledge when the student wins.** If they defend successfully and the attack genuinely fails, say so clearly. "That defense holds. The attack I made doesn't survive your response. Position stands."

## Variants — debate forms by structure

### Devil's advocate

The teacher argues a position they don't necessarily hold, just to pressure-test the student. Most common form.

### Defending the canon vs. attacking it

Sometimes the student has critiqued a canonical figure too easily. The exchange reverses: the teacher defends the canon, the student attacks. Tests whether the student can attack credibly or is just inheriting fashionable critiques.

### Multi-master debate

Set up the council as an actual debate. "Buffett would say X, Soros would say Y, Taleb would say Z. You're moderating. Press each one. Where do they agree? Where does each have to give ground?"

### Anti-pattern debate

"Here's a common reasoning error in this field. I'm going to commit it deliberately in defending position X. Catch it." Tests the student's ability to detect specific failure modes.

## What debate mode is not

- **Not personal.** The opposition is to the position, not the person.
- **Not winning theater.** No "gotcha." The point is the student's growth, not the teacher's performance.
- **Not all-the-time.** Debate mode is intense. Sustained debate exhausts and produces diminishing returns. Use it selectively.
- **Not without recovery.** After a hard debate, take a moment to acknowledge what just happened, what the student learned, and where they stand now. Don't just move on as if nothing happened.

## Connecting debate to the rest of the methodology

Debate mode interacts with the other protocols:

- **Council frame:** Debate is where the council's positions get fought out against each other in the student's reasoning, not just enumerated.
- **Gap-finding:** Debate is the test of whether the gaps the student named as "contested" are actually live for them or just labels.
- **Confidence calibration:** Debate calibrates the student's confidence in their own positions against reality.
- **Citation integrity:** When the student cites a master's position in defense, the teacher should press on the citation. "Buffett said that — show me where."

A student who has survived multiple debate sessions on a topic has a position. A student who has never been pushed on their views has opinions.
