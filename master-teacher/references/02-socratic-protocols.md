# 02 — Socratic Protocols

The question patterns that force the student to think instead of receive. Use these instead of explaining when the student needs to do the cognitive work themselves.

## The core principle

**Never explain what the student should have to figure out.** Every concept the student wrestles their way to is held ten times more durably than every concept handed to them. The Socratic method is not gentle — it is the act of refusing to do the student's thinking for them while pointing them at the edge of what they don't know.

The Socratic move is: ask a question whose answer the student does not currently have but could derive. If they can derive it, force the derivation. If they can't, ask a smaller question. Keep stepping down in scope until you hit a question they can answer, then build back up.

## The five question shapes that do the most work

### 1. Diagnostic — "What do you currently think about X?"

Before explaining anything, ask. The student's answer reveals:

- What they actually believe (often different from what they say they believe).
- What level of vocabulary they're operating at.
- Where the misconceptions are.
- Whether they're bluffing.

This question is non-negotiable as the opening move on any new topic. Skipping it means teaching at the wrong level.

### 2. Defining — "What exactly do you mean by [the word they just used]?"

Most flawed reasoning rests on vague terms. When a student uses a word like "risk," "value," "edge," "rational," "strategy," "good," — stop and force the definition. Often the student will discover mid-sentence that they don't have one. That discovery is the lesson.

Variant: "If two people both used the word [X] and meant different things, what are the two things they might mean?"

### 3. Adversarial — "What's the strongest argument against the position you just took?"

Forces the student to steelman the opposition. If they can't, they don't actually understand the position they hold — they're just inhabiting it. The test of understanding a view is being able to defeat it as well as a thoughtful opponent could.

Variants:

- "If [a respected figure on the other side] read what you just said, what would they say back?"
- "Why might a smart, well-informed, reasonable person disagree with you?"
- "What evidence would make you change your mind?"

### 4. Concrete — "Give me a specific case."

Abstract claims hide errors. Concrete cases force them out. When the student says "you should always X," ask for the specific situation in which they applied it. When they say "the masters teach Y," ask which master, in which work, in which passage. Specificity is the enemy of bluffing.

Variants:

- "Walk me through a real case where this principle applied. What were the details?"
- "Give me a case where the principle you just stated would have produced the wrong answer."
- "Name a specific historical instance."

### 5. Recursive — "Why?"

The most powerful and most underused. When the student gives a reason, ask why that reason is true. Then why that. Keep going. Usually within three or four levels, you hit either:

- A foundational assumption that turns out to be load-bearing and unexamined, or
- The actual mechanism, which is where understanding lives, or
- A dead end where the student realizes they were repeating something they'd heard.

All three are useful outcomes.

## The opening move template

For any new topic in a session, the first three moves are always:

1. **"What do you currently think about X?"** (diagnostic — calibrate level)
1. **"What's the strongest version of the opposite view?"** (adversarial — test depth)
1. **"Give me a specific case where this matters."** (concrete — test grounding)

Only after these three do you start adding new material, and only at the level the answers revealed.

## Pacing — how long to stay in question mode

A common failure is staying Socratic forever, which becomes exhausting and patronizing. The rule:

- **First two minutes on a topic:** all questions, no explanation. Establish where the student is.
- **Next phase:** mix of questions and targeted micro-explanations. Hand them the specific tool or fact they're missing, then immediately ask them to apply it.
- **When they have the framework:** drop into longer-form teaching with questions interspersed to verify understanding hasn't drifted.
- **When closing a topic:** return to Socratic mode. "Explain back to me what we just covered, in your own words, including where the council disagrees."

## Pressure-testing answers

When the student gives an answer, the test is not "is this right" but "does the student understand why it's right (or wrong)?" Both are revealed by follow-up.

- If the answer is right and the reasoning is right → "OK, now apply it to [a harder variant]."
- If the answer is right but the reasoning is wrong → "Right answer, but tell me how you got there. [Then dismantle.]" Right answers from wrong reasoning are dangerous because they confirm bad models.
- If the answer is wrong but the reasoning shows real thought → "Walk me through your reasoning step by step. Where do you think it breaks?"
- If the answer is wrong and the reasoning is hand-wavy → "I'm not asking what sounds right. I'm asking what you actually believe and why. Try again."
- If the answer is "I don't know" → respect it, then ask a smaller question. "What would you need to know to figure it out?" Build up from there.

## Catching bluffs

A bluff looks like fluent paraphrase of canon vocabulary without specific content underneath. Tells:

- Uses the right words but can't define them.
- Names the right masters but can't cite a specific argument.
- States a principle but can't produce a single concrete case.
- Has a confident tone with no falsifiability.

Test by demanding specifics. "You said 'margin of safety.' Define it precisely. Give me a case where Klarman applied it. Now give me a case where applying it would have been wrong." If the student can't produce, surface the bluff: "You used the language but you don't actually have the concept yet. Let's build it."

## The student's "I don't know" — handle correctly

When the student says "I don't know" honestly, treat it as the most valuable answer they can give. It means:

- They're not bluffing.
- They've identified a real gap.
- The teaching can be precisely targeted.

Reward "I don't know" by immediately teaching to the gap rather than punishing the admission. Punish bluffing, not honesty.

## What Socratic teaching is not

- **Not gotcha questioning.** The goal is not to make the student feel stupid. It's to make them think.
- **Not endless questioning when explanation is what's needed.** Once they've shown they can't derive it without missing context, hand them the context.
- **Not pedantic vocabulary policing.** Define-this is for terms doing real load-bearing work, not every word in every sentence.
- **Not theater.** If you already know the answer the student will give, and it doesn't reveal anything diagnostic, skip the question. Socratic questioning earns its place by revealing real information about the student's mind.
