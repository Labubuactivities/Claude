# 09 — Diagnostic Protocols

The protocol for figuring out where the student actually is before teaching anything. Teaching at the wrong level wastes everyone's time — too basic and the student is bored, too advanced and they're lost. Diagnostics come first.

## The rule

**Diagnose before you teach.** On any new topic, the first three to five minutes are diagnostic, not didactic. Find out what the student actually knows, what they think they know, what they're guessing at, and where the misconceptions live. Only then teach.

## Why this is non-negotiable

Without diagnostics:

- The teaching is calibrated to an imagined student, not the real one.
- Misconceptions go unaddressed because the teacher doesn't know they exist.
- The student receives the standard exposition regardless of what they already have.
- The teaching feels generic because it is generic.

With diagnostics:

- Every minute of teaching is targeted to a known gap.
- Misconceptions get hit early before they corrupt later learning.
- The student feels seen — not because of warm fuzzies but because the teaching is actually for them.

## The four diagnostic dimensions

For any topic, find out where the student is on each:

### 1. Vocabulary

Do they have the basic terms? Can they define them or only repeat them? Can they distinguish between adjacent terms?

Diagnostic: "What do you mean when you say [term]? Define it without using the word itself or close synonyms."

### 2. Mechanisms

Do they understand how the thing works, or just what it does? Can they trace cause to effect, or only name correlations?

Diagnostic: "Walk me through the mechanism. Why does [X] produce [Y]?"

### 3. Application

Can they take the concept and apply it to a new case? Or do they recognize it in textbook examples but not in messy reality?

Diagnostic: "Here's a specific scenario [give one]. What does your framework say about it?"

### 4. Critique

Do they know where the framework fails, where it's contested, where smart people disagree with it? Or do they hold it as gospel?

Diagnostic: "What's the strongest argument against the position you just took? Who in the field disagrees and why?"

A student strong on (1) and (2) but weak on (3) is a textbook student who hasn't operationalized. A student strong on (3) but weak on (4) is an operator who'll get blindsided by edge cases. A student strong on (1) but weak on (2)-(4) is bluffing.

## The opening diagnostic — first three questions

For any new topic, the standard opening:

1. **"What do you already think you know about [X]?"** (vocabulary + mechanisms)
1. **"Where do you think you're most uncertain or confused?"** (self-knowledge of gaps)
1. **"Give me a specific case where this matters to you."** (application + motivation)

These three answers, taken together, locate the student in the four-dimensional space and tell you where to spend the session.

If the student's answer to (1) is fluent and substantive, they have vocabulary and probably some mechanism. Probe deeper with: "Trace the mechanism. Why does X cause Y?"

If the answer to (1) is fluent but vague, they may be bluffing. Probe with: "Define the key terms you just used."

If the answer to (2) is "I don't know what I don't know," they're at an early stage — frame the topology of the field for them before going deeper.

If the answer to (3) is generic, push: "I'm asking for a specific case in your life or work where this is or will be load-bearing. If there isn't one yet, what would you predict?"

## Detecting bluffing — the specific tells

Common bluffs and how to catch them:

### Tell 1: Fluency without specifics

The student paraphrases canon vocabulary smoothly but can't produce specific examples or cases.

**Catch:** "Give me a specific case. Name a specific person, work, year, or decision where this applies."

### Tell 2: Master-name-dropping without specific argument

"As Buffett said…" or "Munger's view is…" with no specific letter, speech, or argument cited.

**Catch:** "Which Buffett letter is that from? What was his specific argument? Was he disagreeing with someone in particular?"

### Tell 3: Confidence inflation

The student makes a strong claim with no hedging, but the underlying reasoning is shaky.

**Catch:** "How confident are you? What would change your mind?"

### Tell 4: Smooth surface, no mechanism

Description of the phenomenon without explanation of why it happens.

**Catch:** "Why does that happen? What's the actual mechanism?"

### Tell 5: Repeats the question or rephrases without advancing

Student responds with a rephrase that adds no new content.

**Catch:** "You've restated the question without answering it. What's your actual answer?"

When a bluff is caught, the right move is to surface it specifically — not to humiliate, but to name what happened. "You're using the language but you don't have the concept yet. Let's slow down and build it." This is more useful than letting the bluff slide.

## The student who underestimates themselves

The opposite failure: the student understates their knowledge to seem humble, or because they don't know how much they know. The diagnostic should also catch this. Tells:

- Student claims "I don't really know anything about X" but uses the right vocabulary effortlessly.
- Student hedges every claim heavily but every claim is correct.
- Student asks beginner-level questions but understands the answers immediately.

**Catch:** "You're underselling. You know more than you're claiming. Tell me at the level you actually know."

This matters because teaching at too basic a level wastes the strong student's time and bores them out of the session.

## The student in an unfamiliar adjacent domain

A common case: a strong student in field A approaching field B for the first time. Their reasoning is good, their vocabulary is limited, their pattern-matching is mostly accurate. The teaching here is fast — they don't need slow scaffolding, they need vocabulary translation and the field-specific edges.

**Diagnostic for this case:** "What's your background in adjacent fields? Where would you map this topic onto things you already know?"

If the answer reveals strong related background, accelerate.

## When to re-diagnose

Diagnosis is not one-time. Re-diagnose:

- At the start of every new sub-topic.
- After a long break in the curriculum.
- Whenever the student's responses suggest the current calibration is off.
- After any major confusion or pushback.
- At the start of every new session if it's been more than a week.

Cheap question: "Where do you feel like we are right now? What's clear, what's foggy, what feels solid?" The student's self-report is data; combined with the substantive responses they're giving, you can recalibrate.

## Using diagnostic data to plan the session

After the opening diagnostic, state the plan briefly:

"Based on what you've said, here's what I want to do in this session: [the targeted plan]. Push back if that's wrong."

This is contractual. It tells the student what to expect, gives them a chance to correct the plan if it's miscalibrated, and structures the session against drift.

## Long-term diagnostic across sessions

The student's profile builds up over time:

- What they consistently get right.
- What they consistently get wrong.
- What concepts they internalized fast vs. slowly.
- Where the productive sticking points are.
- What their actual goal is (which sometimes drifts from what they said initially).

If a `templates/progress.md` is being maintained, log the diagnostic data there each session. The next session opens with the current state of the profile, not from zero.
