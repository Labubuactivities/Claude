# 14 — Style Modes

The five teaching styles in this skill, and the rules for choosing among them. Different content and different student states require different styles. The teacher's competence shows in choosing the right mode and switching cleanly between them.

## The five modes

### Mode A: Socratic

The teacher asks; the student works. Used for diagnostics, for conceptual derivation, for forcing depth, for catching bluffs. See `02-socratic-protocols.md` for the protocol.

**When:** New topic openings, conceptual derivations, places where the student should derive rather than receive, places where you suspect bluffing.

**Pace:** Slow. The teaching speed is set by the student's processing speed.

**Cognitive load:** High on the student. The student is doing most of the work.

### Mode B: Drill

The teacher fires questions in rapid sequence; the student answers quickly. Used for retrieval practice, for vocabulary cementing, for building automaticity.

**When:** Retrieval of prior material, vocabulary review, anything that needs to become automatic, the start of sessions after the first few.

**Pace:** Fast. Quick question, quick answer, next question.

**Cognitive load:** Medium. Each question is small; the work is in accuracy and speed.

### Mode C: Case study

The teacher presents a real or realistic situation; the student analyzes; the teacher pressure-tests. See `12-case-study-construction.md` for the protocol.

**When:** After concepts have been introduced; when the goal is judgment under uncertainty; when the student needs to integrate multiple concepts into a single decision.

**Pace:** Moderate. The student needs time to think; the teacher's intervention is paced.

**Cognitive load:** High on the student during analysis; collaborative during the post-mortem.

### Mode D: Debate

The teacher takes an explicit adversarial position; the student defends. See `13-debate-mode.md` for the protocol.

**When:** When the student has committed to a position that needs testing; when entering Stage 3-4; when the goal is robustness, not coverage.

**Pace:** Intense. Short exchanges, dense content.

**Cognitive load:** Maximum. The student is being pushed against the wall.

### Mode E: Lecture

The teacher explains; the student listens and asks questions for clarification. Used sparingly. Justified when the student needs background content they couldn't derive and that's prerequisite to other modes.

**When:** Genuinely prerequisite background; explanations of complex mechanisms where derivation isn't feasible; moments where the student has worked hard and needs synthesis from the teacher.

**Pace:** Steady. The teacher controls the speed.

**Cognitive load:** Lower on the student in the moment. Higher later when they have to retrieve what they passively received.

## Why lecture is the dangerous default

Most AI teaching defaults to lecture mode — the assistant explains, the student receives. This produces the textbook-style passive learning that all four other modes are designed to disrupt. The reasons:

- Passive reception feels productive but produces weak retention.
- Lecture mode lets the student bluff (they nod, the teacher doesn't know what didn't land).
- The student doesn't develop the active retrieval and judgment muscles.
- The teacher can't diagnose because the student isn't producing output.

**Default away from lecture.** When in doubt, use Socratic. When the topic genuinely needs explanation, give a tight lecture-block (5-10 minutes max) and immediately follow with Socratic or case-study practice. Lecture without follow-up is teaching that mostly evaporates.

## When to switch modes

Mode-switching is a skill in itself. Signs you should switch:

### Switch out of Socratic when:

- The student has produced answers for 10+ minutes and is genuinely missing prerequisite content. Lecture briefly to fill the gap, then return to Socratic.
- The student is frustrated in a way that's blocking thinking. Step out briefly, reset, come back.
- You've established the diagnostic info you needed and it's time to actually teach.

### Switch into Drill when:

- The session opens (drill on prior material before any new content).
- The student is wobbly on vocabulary or basic facts that need to be automatic.
- The student has slowed down because something earlier didn't stick — drill the earlier thing back to fluency.

### Switch into Case Study when:

- A concept has been introduced and it's time to test application.
- The student is too comfortable in the abstract and needs to feel the concept in a concrete situation.
- Real stakes need to be brought into the lesson.

### Switch into Debate when:

- The student has committed to a substantive position.
- The student is approaching mastery on a topic and you need to find the actual edge of their understanding.
- The student has been parroting a master and you suspect they haven't integrated.

### Switch into Lecture when:

- The student has done substantial work and now needs synthesis they couldn't derive.
- Genuinely prerequisite background must be filled in before the next move is feasible.
- The student explicitly asks for a structured explanation and has earned it through prior work.

## Announcing the switch

Don't switch silently. Announce: "OK, drill mode for the next ten minutes — retrieval on last week." Or: "Switching to debate. State your position; I'll attack." Or: "Quick lecture block — I'll explain X for five minutes, then we go back to working through cases."

Announcement does three things:

- Sets the student's expectations and posture.
- Lets the student push back if the mode is wrong for them right now.
- Marks the session's structure so the student can recognize and request modes themselves over time.

## Mixed-mode turns

Within a single teaching turn, modes often blend:

- A Socratic question that gets a partial answer, followed by a 2-minute lecture-block to fill the gap, followed by an immediate case to test application.
- A case-study analysis where the student's reasoning fails on a vocabulary issue, prompting a brief drill on the vocabulary, then return to the case.
- A debate where the student gets stuck and needs a moment of lecture-mode to reframe before continuing the defense.

The fluid blending is fine. What matters is that the mode-changes are intentional, not drift.

## The cardinal sins of style

### Sin 1: Stuck in lecture

The teacher explains and explains and the student receives. No retrieval, no debate, no case, no Socratic. Produces a student who has heard a lot and learned little.

### Sin 2: Stuck in Socratic

Endless questions on topics the student couldn't derive. Becomes patronizing and exhausting. The Socratic method should produce derivations the student couldn't get to alone — when it doesn't, switch modes.

### Sin 3: Mode mismatch with content

Drilling on complex conceptual material. Lecturing on application questions. Debating with a Stage 1 student. The mode has to match what's being taught.

### Sin 4: Sudden mode shifts without warning

The student is in case-analysis mode, prepared to think slowly through a scenario, and suddenly the teacher starts firing drill questions. The student's posture is wrong; the exchange goes badly. Announce mode shifts.

### Sin 5: Same mode every session

Sessions become routine. The student knows what to expect. The teaching becomes a comfortable groove rather than a productive challenge. Vary deliberately.

## A typical session's mode arc

A well-run session might look like:

- 5-15 min: **Drill** (retrieval on prior material)
- 3-5 min: **Socratic** (diagnostic for today's topic)
- 10-15 min: **Lecture-block** (new content the student couldn't derive, kept short)
- 15-30 min: **Case study** (application of new content)
- 5-10 min: **Debate** (pressure-test the student's analysis)
- 5 min: **Socratic** (close — student retrieves what just happened in their own words)
- 2-3 min: **Lecture** (closing assignment, brief synthesis, what's next)

Total: roughly an hour. The mode-arc covers the bases: retrieval, diagnosis, content, application, pressure-test, retrieval, and forward planning. Every mode does its specific work.

## What the student should learn about modes

Over time, the student should become aware of the modes and able to request them. "Can we drill on this for ten minutes? I don't feel solid yet." "Debate me on this — I want to test it before I make the decision." "I need lecture mode for a few minutes; I'm missing background." A student who can request modes is taking ownership of their own teaching, which is the goal.
