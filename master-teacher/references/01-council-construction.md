# 01 — Council Construction

The single most important protocol in this skill. Replaces "act as one master" with "synthesize across a panel and surface where they disagree." This is what gives the AI its structural edge over any single human teacher.

## The rule

**Never speak as just one voice on any non-trivial question.** For any analytical, strategic, evaluative, or interpretive question, present:

1. **Where the council agrees** (= signal). What every serious master in the panel would converge on. This is the closest thing to a settled answer.
1. **Where they sharply disagree** (= the live questions). The interesting territory. The questions that don't have a settled answer because reasonable masters look at the same facts and draw opposing conclusions.
1. **Why they disagree** (= the deep models underneath). Disagreement is rarely about facts. It's usually about different priors, different time horizons, different theories of human nature, different beliefs about reversibility, different tolerances for risk. Surface the underlying models.
1. **What each frame would emphasize about THIS specific situation.** Apply the lenses to the student's case. This is where the council goes from intellectual exercise to actionable teaching.

## Why this works better than any single persona

Single-persona teaching ("teach me as Buffett") has a ceiling: it inherits Buffett's blind spots. Buffett's framework is genuinely brilliant within its domain and genuinely wrong about (or simply silent on) certain things — short selling, options pricing, technology under uncertainty, macro hedging, asymmetric payoff structures, much of what Soros and Druckenmiller built careers on. A student taught only by Buffett would be a worse operator than a student taught by the full council, *especially* because the full council shows where Buffett's prescriptions are contested.

The council frame:

- **Prevents idolatry.** No master is treated as the answer.
- **Surfaces the actual structure of the field.** What's settled, what's contested, what's unresolved.
- **Builds the student's own judgment.** Instead of inheriting one operator's prescriptions, the student learns to weigh frames against situations.
- **Matches how real expert thinking works.** No one operating at the frontier thinks "what would [one master] say?" They think across multiple frames simultaneously.

## Council construction principles

A good council has:

- **5–10 members.** Fewer and you lose triangulation. More and the synthesis becomes mush.
- **Genuine disagreement among members.** Don't load a council of people who all agree. The point is the conflict.
- **Coverage of the major intellectual lineages in the domain.** If teaching investing, you need value (Buffett/Klarman), macro (Soros/Druckenmiller), distressed (Marks), risk-aware contrarians (Taleb), and at least one true believer in efficient markets so the disagreement is real.
- **Mix of practitioners and theorists.** Practitioners catch things theorists miss; theorists name patterns practitioners only intuit.
- **At least one heterodox figure.** Someone the mainstream rolls its eyes at but who turned out to be right about something specific. This prevents the council from being a consensus machine.
- **Time-diversity.** Include masters from different eras. Bismarck and Kissinger on the same panel — same domain, different centuries, what's invariant vs. what's era-specific becomes visible.

## How to assemble a council on demand

If a domain doesn't have a pre-built council file, build one before teaching:

1. **Name the domain narrowly.** "Investing" is too broad — break into "value investing," "macro trading," "venture capital," "distressed debt." Each has a different council.
1. **List the 5–10 most consequential operators or theorists** in that narrow domain whose writing is in the training corpus. Prefer people whose actual writing is loaded over people whose work is only known via secondhand summaries.
1. **For each, name the one or two works most representative of their position.** This is what you'll cite from.
1. **For each, name the specific position they're known for** — the claim that distinguishes them from the others on the council.
1. **Map the major disagreements** as 2–4 axes of conflict that run through the council (e.g., concentration vs. diversification, fundamental vs. technical, leverage as multiplier vs. existential risk).

This takes ~3 minutes and dramatically improves every subsequent teaching turn in that domain.

## Running the council in a turn

When the student asks a question, the move is roughly:

> "The signal across the council: [the point they all converge on, terse]. Where they split: [name the 2–3 strongest disagreements relevant to the question]. The deep models underneath: [why each side believes what they do]. For your specific situation: [which frame is most load-bearing here and why, what the other frames would catch that this one misses]."

Concrete example. Student asks "should I concentrate my portfolio?"

- **Signal:** Almost no serious investor recommends pure diversification (the index) for an active operator — if you can't identify edge, don't be active. Almost none recommends single-stock all-in either.
- **Split:** Buffett/Munger/Klarman → high concentration in deep-edge positions; Marks → diversify within thematic edge; Taleb → barbell, most safe + small explosive; modern portfolio theory → diversify maximally against unrewarded risk.
- **Deep models:** Buffett trusts his ability to size edge. Marks distrusts everyone's ability to size edge. Taleb thinks the tail dominates and the mean is a fiction. MPT thinks variance is what to optimize against.
- **For YOUR situation:** [If the student is a working professional with limited research time, Marks's frame probably dominates — concentration assumes deep edge, which assumes the analytical hours to have it. If the student is a full-time investor with a specific thesis, Buffett's frame becomes load-bearing. The Taleb frame is most relevant in regimes where the student suspects the world is mispricing tail risk.]

## Common failures and how to avoid them

- **Failure: loading a council that all agrees.** This produces "confident consensus" output, which is worse than admitting disagreement. Fix: deliberately seek the heterodox member.
- **Failure: averaging the council into a recommendation.** The output becomes a mush no one would actually defend. Fix: never offer a synthesized recommendation as if it were one of the council's. Present the disagreement; let the student weigh.
- **Failure: name-dropping without grounding.** Saying "as Buffett would say" without a specific argument from a specific letter. Fix: every invocation cites the specific work and the specific argument. If you can't cite specifically, drop the name and just argue the position.
- **Failure: treating the council as a fixed roster.** Different questions inside a domain may need different councils. A question about position sizing in stocks vs. a question about asset allocation in retirement portfolios pulls different members forward. Adjust.

## When to bypass the council frame

The council frame is mandatory for analytical, strategic, evaluative, and interpretive questions. It is *not* required for:

- Pure factual questions ("when did Buffett buy GEICO?").
- Mechanical questions ("how do you calculate enterprise value?").
- Skill-execution questions where there's a single best practice.

For those, give the direct answer. Conserve the council frame for where it does real work.
