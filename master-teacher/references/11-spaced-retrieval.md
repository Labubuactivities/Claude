# 11 — Spaced Retrieval

The protocol that fights the dominant force in long-term learning: forgetting. New material gets covered, feels learned, fades. Without retrieval practice, most teaching is wasted. This file specifies how to keep prior material alive.

## The rule

**Every session begins with retrieval practice on prior material.** Not optional. Not skippable. Not replaced by "let me know if you have questions about last time." The first 5-15 minutes of every session is the student being quizzed on what they covered before.

## Why this works

The empirical literature on retrieval practice (Roediger, Karpicke, and the broader cognitive psychology of memory) shows clearly:

- **Active recall beats passive review.** Trying to retrieve a memory strengthens it more than re-reading it.
- **Spacing beats massing.** Retrievals spread over time produce dramatically more durable memory than retrievals clustered together.
- **Effortful retrieval beats easy retrieval.** A retrieval that takes work to produce is more retention-effective than one that comes quickly.
- **Errors during retrieval are productive if corrected.** Students who get an answer wrong and are then corrected remember better than students who never tried.

This means: a session that ends with "did you learn that?" is far less effective than one that begins three days later with "OK, tell me what we covered last time about [X]." The second design uses forgetting as the engine of long-term retention.

## The retrieval schedule

A working schedule for spaced retrieval:

- **End of every session:** Quick recap by the student, in their own words.
- **Beginning of next session (1-3 days later):** Full retrieval practice on the prior session's content.
- **One week after a topic was covered:** Brief return — one or two questions to keep it warm.
- **One month after:** Another brief return.
- **Three months after:** Brief check — does it still hold?
- **Whenever the topic becomes relevant in a new session:** Re-activate by connecting old material to new.

This decays exponentially in frequency, which matches the forgetting curve.

## The opening retrieval — how to run it

The session opens with the student doing the cognitive work. Move:

> "Before we start today: tell me what we covered last time. Not a summary — actually walk me through the substantive content. What were the main concepts? Where did the council disagree? What was the case study? What did you conclude?"

The student talks. The teacher listens. Then:

> "OK. Now — what did you find yourself unsure of since? Where did the material feel solid and where did it feel slippery?"

This produces:

- A retrieval workout for the student.
- Diagnostic information about what stuck.
- A specific list of things to reinforce before adding new material.

If the student blanks: do not bail them out instantly. Make them work for it. "Take a minute. What do you remember?" If they're still stuck after a minute, prompt with the smallest possible hint, then have them continue.

## Question types for retrieval

Vary the question type to test different facets of memory:

- **Definition retrieval:** "Define [term]."
- **Mechanism retrieval:** "Why does [X] cause [Y]?"
- **Master/position retrieval:** "Which master held [position]? What was their argument?"
- **Counter retrieval:** "What was the strongest opposing view we discussed?"
- **Case retrieval:** "Walk me through the case we used to illustrate this."
- **Application retrieval:** "How would this apply to [novel case]?"
- **Connection retrieval:** "We connected this to [other domain]. How did the analogy go?"
- **Gap retrieval:** "Where did we say the canonical position is contested or wrong?"

Rotate. A student quizzed only on definitions will get good at definitions and stay weak on mechanisms.

## When the student fails a retrieval

This is the most teaching-rich moment in the session. The student has just demonstrated that something they thought they knew didn't stick. Don't waste it.

The wrong move: "OK no problem, here it is again [recap the material]."

The right move:

1. Notice the failure specifically. "You're blanking on the mechanism part. The definition is there but the why isn't."
1. Probe what they do have. "Tell me what you remember — even partial."
1. Pull the rest from the student wherever possible. "If [X] then… what?"
1. Only fill in what they truly can't reconstruct.
1. Set a re-retrieval test for the next session on this specific failed piece.

Failed retrievals, when handled well, produce dramatically stronger memory than easy retrievals. They're a feature, not a bug.

## Spacing across topics

When the curriculum covers multiple topics, retrieval should interleave them rather than block them. Specifically:

- Within a session, occasionally pull a question from a topic covered 2-3 sessions ago, not just the most recent.
- After 5-10 sessions, occasionally pull a question from the very early sessions.
- After a topic feels "complete," return to it months later with a hard question.

Interleaving forces the student to first identify which framework applies, then apply it. Blocked practice lets them assume the framework — and assumption is what fails them in real situations where the framework isn't pre-identified.

## The student's own retrieval practice between sessions

Encourage the student to self-quiz between sessions. Specifically:

- After each session, write down 3-5 questions covering the session's content. Try to answer them a few days later, before opening notes.
- Read the canonical works actively — close the book at intervals and try to articulate what was just said.
- Try to teach the material to someone else (or to a journal). Teaching forces retrieval.

This compounds with in-session retrieval to produce durable mastery.

## The mistake of treating retrieval as a chore

Retrieval feels harder than re-reading. Students often resist it because it surfaces what they don't remember and feels worse than just being told again. This is the same psychology as not weighing yourself when on a diet — avoiding the data feels better short-term and is worse long-term.

The teaching move: name this dynamic explicitly the first time the student resists. "Retrieval feels harder than re-reading. That's because it works. The discomfort is the signal that memory is being strengthened. Push through."

After they've experienced the benefits (typically 3-5 sessions in), the resistance fades. The student notices that material they retrieved is dramatically more accessible than material they just heard.

## Connecting retrieval to assessment

If the student wants formal markers of progress, retrieval doubles as assessment:

- Track which questions they answered fully, partially, or not at all.
- Note the topics where retrieval is consistently weak — those are the curriculum gaps.
- Use the trajectory of retrieval performance over months as the actual measure of mastery, not the perceived smoothness of any single session.

A student who can retrieve 80% of material from three months ago is dramatically further along than a student who could answer 100% of questions immediately after the original session but can't retrieve 30% three months later. The second student felt expert at the time and isn't.

## Retrieval as humility

The most important meta-effect: students who practice retrieval discover, repeatedly, that they know less than they thought they knew. This is uncomfortable and absolutely essential. The student who has been disabused of their own confident illusions of knowledge becomes a different kind of operator — one who actually checks before betting. That's worth far more than the specific content retained.
