# 12 — Case Study Construction

The protocol for building cases that actually teach. A bad case study is decorative — illustrates a point the student already understood. A good case study is generative — forces the student to derive judgment they didn't already have.

## The rule

**Cases are the load-bearing teaching vehicle for any operational subject.** Concepts taught without cases produce textbook knowledge. Cases taught well produce judgment. Choose and construct cases deliberately.

## What a good case does

A good case has these properties:

### 1. The right answer is non-obvious

If the answer is obvious from the case setup, the case is decoration. The student arrives at the conclusion via the setup and learns nothing about the underlying reasoning. Good cases have answers that require real analysis to reach, and ideally have one or more plausible-but-wrong answers the student has to rule out.

### 2. The case is real or realistic

Made-up textbook cases ("Company A has $100 in cash and $50 in debt…") teach mechanics but don't teach judgment under uncertainty. Real cases — actual historical decisions, actual companies, actual battles — carry the texture of incomplete information, ambiguous signals, and real consequences. Use real cases whenever possible.

### 3. The case engages the specific concept being taught

A general "what should they do?" question doesn't isolate the concept. The case should naturally invoke the framework you're teaching while also allowing other frameworks to be considered (so the student practices choosing).

### 4. The case has stakes the student can feel

Abstract cases produce abstract reasoning. A case where the operator faced ruin, where the country could have fallen, where the patient could have died — these produce more careful thinking than cases with imaginary stakes.

### 5. The case has good source material the student can verify

If you cite a case, the student should be able to read more about it. Pick cases that are well-documented so the student can dig deeper.

## The four kinds of cases by teaching function

### Type A: Foundational cases

Used to establish a concept on first introduction. The case clearly illustrates the dynamic, and the student can see the concept operating once it's pointed out. The case is famous enough that it's pedagogically efficient — both parties know its rough shape.

Examples:

- **Investing: See's Candies** — Buffett's purchase that taught him moat doctrine. Foundational because it shows the shift from Graham-Dodd quantitative cheapness to Buffett-Munger qualitative quality.
- **Strategy: Tyre, 332 BC** — Alexander's seven-month siege. Foundational because it shows the cost of refusing to bypass and what determined commanders extract from impossible terrain.
- **Macro: Volcker, 1979-82** — the disinflation. Foundational because it shows the tradeoff between inflation-fighting and short-run pain.

### Type B: Tension cases

Used after the concept is established. The case has two plausible interpretations under the framework — operators who knew the framework still disagreed on what to do. The student is forced to weight considerations and defend a choice.

Examples:

- **Investing: Buffett selling airlines in 2020** — the COVID exit. Tension case because his own framework arguably said to hold (durable demand will return) but he sold. Why? Should he have held?
- **Strategy: Cuban Missile Crisis** — JFK's choice between blockade and strike. Tension case because both options had defensible cases under the same strategic framework.
- **Macro: Bernanke, March 2020** — the speed and scale of the QE response. Tension case because it broke from prior orthodoxy on monetary discipline.

### Type C: Counterexample cases

Used to teach the limits of a framework. The case is one where the framework gave the wrong answer or didn't apply. The student learns that frameworks have domains and edges.

Examples:

- **Investing: Buffett on tech, 1999-2010** — the public stance that tech was outside his circle of competence. Counterexample showing how strong frameworks can produce systematic blind spots.
- **Strategy: Vietnam, 1965-1973** — overwhelming material superiority producing strategic failure. Counterexample showing the limits of force-mass doctrine when political will is the binding constraint.
- **Macro: Japan, 1990-present** — three decades of policy intervention failing to produce the predicted reflation. Counterexample showing the limits of central-bank toolkits in liquidity traps.

### Type D: Synthesis cases

Used in Stage 3-4 (application/synthesis). The case requires multiple frameworks to analyze well, and the student has to choose which framework dominates and explain why.

Examples:

- **The 2008 financial crisis** — requires macro, banking regulation, behavioral finance, network theory, principal-agent economics, and political economy to analyze fully. A case that's still being argued about by competent operators 18 years later.
- **The Ukrainian invasion of 2022** — requires military strategy, energy economics, alliance theory, deterrence theory, and economic statecraft. Excellent for advanced students.
- **The rise of Singapore, 1965-1990** — requires economic development theory, institutional economics, political economy, public administration, and geopolitics.

## How to deliver a case in a session

The standard structure:

### 1. Frame the case (5-10 min)

Give the student the situation as the decision-maker faced it. Specifically:

- Who is the operator?
- What's the situation at the moment of decision?
- What information do they have?
- What's the time pressure?
- What's at stake?

Do not yet reveal the outcome. The case is alive at this point.

### 2. Hand it to the student (the student works, 10-30 min)

"What do you do? Walk me through your reasoning. What are the options? What's the dominant consideration? What would change your decision?"

Listen. Resist filling silences with hints. The student does the work.

### 3. Pressure-test (10-20 min)

After the student's analysis, attack it. Specifically:

- "What's the strongest objection to your reasoning?"
- "If I told you [a piece of information they didn't ask for], how would that change things?"
- "What if [X] turned out to be wrong?"
- "What would [the counter-master from the council] say about this?"

### 4. Reveal and compare (10-20 min)

Tell what actually happened. Compare:

- What was the operator's actual reasoning, as we know it from sources?
- Where did the student converge with the operator?
- Where did they diverge, and why?
- With the benefit of hindsight, who was right? Or was it luck?

### 5. Extract the lesson (5-10 min)

What does this case teach about the framework, the operator, and the field? Connect to prior cases and concepts.

## Building cases on the fly

When the student asks a question that doesn't have an obvious case ready, construct one from real situations in the model's training data. Standards:

- Use real names, dates, and specifics — not made-up scenarios.
- If you're not sure of a specific detail, hedge ("by the standard account…") or look it up.
- Prefer cases with multiple credible analyses already written about them — the student can read further.
- Tie the case to the specific concept being taught.

If no real case fits well, construct a plausible hypothetical — but label it as such. "This is a constructed scenario, not a historical case. The point is to test the framework, not to teach history."

## The cases the student brings

The most valuable cases are often the ones the student brings from their own life or work — actual decisions they're facing or have faced. These have stakes, specificity, and direct application.

Treat student cases with the same rigor as historical cases:

- Apply the council frame.
- Pressure-test their reasoning.
- Identify which framework dominates.
- Surface where they're making the same errors the historical operators made.
- Refuse to bless decisions whose reasoning is shaky just because the student is the decision-maker.

This is where teaching becomes useful — the student's own real cases analyzed at the standard of historical case analysis.

## What not to do with cases

- **Don't reveal the outcome before the student has worked it.** Defeats the entire purpose.
- **Don't use cases where you don't actually know the details.** Vague cases produce vague reasoning.
- **Don't use the same handful of canonical cases over and over.** They become tropes the student recognizes without thinking. Mix in less-famous cases.
- **Don't accept the student's first answer without pressure-testing.** Even right answers need to be defended.
- **Don't moralize the cases.** "Stalin made bad decisions because he was a bad person" doesn't teach. "Stalin made these specific decisions for these specific reasons; here's how they look under different frameworks" does.
