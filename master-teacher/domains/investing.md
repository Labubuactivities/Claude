# Domain: Investing

The council, canon, and live debates for serious investing education. Load this when the invocation is "use master-teacher investing" or when the topic is clearly investment-focused.

## The council

Ten members chosen for genuine internal disagreement, time-diversity, and complementary frames. This is not a list of "great investors" — it's a list of people whose specific positions on contested questions force productive debate.

### 1. Warren Buffett

**Position:** Concentrated, qualitative, long-duration value investing. Moat doctrine. Buy great businesses at fair prices and hold.

**Canonical sources:** The Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters (1965-present), especially 1977, 1989 (margin of safety), 1996 (durable competitive advantage / four moat sources), 1999 (cash and tech), 2014 (50-year retrospective).

**Where to press him:** Why does the moat doctrine fail to identify tech platforms ex ante (1990s-2000s record)? How do you distinguish ex ante from ex post moat identification? Why did position sizing not protect against the airline exit?

### 2. Charlie Munger

**Position:** Buffett's intellectual partner. Multidisciplinary mental models. The willingness to do nothing for long stretches. More willing than Buffett to acknowledge moat erosion and qualitative judgment difficulty.

**Canonical sources:** *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (the speeches), *Damn Right!* (the Janet Lowe biography), the Daily Journal annual meeting transcripts, the USC Law School commencement speech (2007), the Harvard Law School "Psychology of Human Misjudgment" speech (1995, revised later).

**Where to press him:** The latticework of mental models — is this actually how he thinks, or a post-hoc rationalization of a more intuitive process? Where did multidisciplinary thinking specifically pay off vs. cost him?

### 3. Seth Klarman

**Position:** Deep value with explicit margin of safety. Cash as a position. Willing to wait years. Operates in distressed and special situations more than Buffett.

**Canonical sources:** *Margin of Safety* (1991, out of print but well-circulated), Baupost shareholder letters, occasional public talks.

**Where to press him:** The 1990s and 2010s gave you long periods where the deep-value framework produced underperformance. How do you distinguish "the framework is in a hard period" from "the framework has been arbitraged away"?

### 4. Howard Marks

**Position:** Cycle awareness over stock selection. Memos that emphasize where you are in the cycle as the primary determinant of forward returns. Distressed credit specialization.

**Canonical sources:** The Oaktree Memos (1990-present, all available on Oaktree's website), *The Most Important Thing* (2011), *Mastering the Market Cycle* (2018).

**Where to press him:** Cycle theory has more predictive power for credit than equity, more for the medium term than the short term. How do you actually operate when "we're late cycle" has been true for five years?

### 5. George Soros

**Position:** Reflexivity. Markets don't just process information about fundamentals — they shape the fundamentals through belief feedback loops. Macro speculation on regime changes.

**Canonical sources:** *The Alchemy of Finance* (1987), *Soros on Soros* (1995), *The New Paradigm for Financial Markets* (2008), various essays and interviews.

**Where to press him:** Reflexivity is hard to operationalize except in retrospect. How do you actually trade reflexively in advance? What's the difference between "reflexive feedback loop" and "I'm guessing about narratives"?

### 6. Stanley Druckenmiller

**Position:** Concentrated macro speculation. Pattern recognition over models. Willingness to put on enormous positions when conviction is high. Liquidity awareness.

**Canonical sources:** No book — but extensive interviews (the 2015 Lost Tree Club talk, various Bloomberg conversations, the Duquesne Family Office commentary), commentary across many trading-floor profiles.

**Where to press him:** Pattern recognition is non-falsifiable. How do you tell a pattern from coincidence? How much of the record is luck vs. skill, given the small number of mega-positions?

### 7. Nassim Nicholas Taleb

**Position:** Tails dominate the average; the Gaussian framework systematically underestimates rare events; survivorship bias contaminates most success narratives. Barbell strategy. Antifragility.

**Canonical sources:** *Fooled by Randomness* (2001), *The Black Swan* (2007), *Antifragile* (2012), *Skin in the Game* (2018), the technical *Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails* (2020).

**Where to press him:** The framework predicts disasters in retrospect more reliably than in advance. How does an operator actually act on "tails dominate" without paying the carry costs of perpetual tail hedging? What's the empirical record on Taleb-style strategies?

### 8. Ray Dalio

**Position:** Diversified all-weather portfolio construction. Macro template based on debt cycles. Principles-based approach to investing and management.

**Canonical sources:** *Principles* (2017), *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises* (2018), *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* (2021), Bridgewater Daily Observations (proprietary), various YouTube content.

**Where to press him:** The empirical record of all-weather since publication is mixed. Are the principles a model of how markets work or a model of how Dalio reasons? How much of Bridgewater's record is the macro thesis vs. specific positioning skill?

### 9. Benjamin Graham

**Position:** Quantitative deep value. Buy below intrinsic worth, defined statistically. Mr. Market as metaphor. Margin of safety as concept.

**Canonical sources:** *Security Analysis* (1934, with Dodd), *The Intelligent Investor* (1949), articles in the *Financial Analysts Journal*.

**Where to press him:** Most of the original Graham formulas don't work today (the quantitative cheapness arbitrage has been hammered). The conceptual framework survived; the specific operating rules largely didn't. Where's the line?

### 10. Eugene Fama / Efficient Markets

**Position:** Markets are largely efficient in the weak/semi-strong sense; persistent alpha is rare and usually attributable to factor exposures (size, value, momentum, quality, low-volatility). The base assumption should be that beating the market is hard.

**Canonical sources:** Fama's academic papers (1965 dissertation, the 1992 Fama-French three-factor, the 2015 five-factor extensions), books surveying the literature.

**Where to press him:** The factor zoo problem — too many factors, post-hoc fitting. How do you reconcile the existence of consistent multi-decade outperformers (Buffett, Klarman, Renaissance) with the EMH? Is the answer just "luck"?

## The major axes of disagreement

These run through the council and structure most contested questions.

### Axis 1: Concentration vs. diversification

- **Concentrate:** Buffett, Munger, Klarman, Druckenmiller (in his macro book)
- **Diversify within edge:** Marks, Soros (across positions, concentrated within)
- **Maximally diversify:** Fama, modern portfolio theory
- **Barbell (most safe + small explosive):** Taleb
- **All-weather across asset classes:** Dalio

### Axis 2: Time horizon

- **Multi-decade:** Buffett, Munger, Klarman
- **Multi-year, cycle-aware:** Marks, Dalio
- **Months to years on regime trades:** Soros, Druckenmiller
- **Indifferent to horizon (factor-driven):** Fama
- **Asymmetric: short on downside, long on upside:** Taleb

### Axis 3: Process — qualitative judgment vs. quantitative rule

- **Qualitative:** Buffett, Munger, Klarman
- **Quantitative + qualitative:** Marks, Dalio
- **Pure pattern recognition:** Druckenmiller, Soros (with elaborate theory layered on)
- **Pure quantitative:** Fama, Renaissance
- **Quantitative on tails, qualitative on selection:** Taleb

### Axis 4: Market efficiency

- **Mostly inefficient, dedicated operators can extract returns:** Buffett, Klarman, Druckenmiller, Marks
- **Reflexively non-efficient, the market shapes the world it tries to predict:** Soros
- **Efficient in the price-information sense; factor-explanable:** Fama
- **Pseudo-efficient until it isn't; tails dominate:** Taleb
- **Efficient enough that selection is hard; macro and asset allocation matter more:** Dalio

### Axis 5: Leverage

- **Avoid:** Buffett, Munger, Klarman (mostly)
- **Use within tight risk frames:** Marks, Druckenmiller
- **Use heavily on conviction:** Soros (historically)
- **Use only on the upside leg of the barbell:** Taleb
- **Use within portfolio construction for risk parity:** Dalio

## Curriculum sequence

Suggested progression from Stage 1 to Stage 4:

### Stage 1: Orientation

- The Graham → Buffett → Munger lineage; what was new at each step.
- The value vs. growth false dichotomy (and Buffett's resolution that "growth is a component of value").
- The efficient markets challenge and the partial responses.
- The basic vocabulary: intrinsic value, margin of safety, moat, mean reversion, factor.

### Stage 2: Mechanism

- Why moats work and how they erode. Specific case work on See's Candies, Coca-Cola, IBM, Apple, Kraft Heinz, the airlines.
- Reflexivity in practice: the GBP-DEM trade 1992, the late-90s bubble, 2007-08 credit, the 2020-21 SPAC cycle.
- Cycle dynamics: credit cycles vs. equity cycles vs. sentiment cycles.
- Why tails matter: the empirical fat-tail evidence in returns, the carry-cost of hedging, the failure of VaR models.
- The factor literature: what's been arbitraged away, what hasn't, why momentum is the most empirically robust factor.

### Stage 3: Application

- Real position analyses. The student brings actual investment ideas and works through them with the council frame.
- Historical decision cases: Buffett's 1980 GEICO doubling-down; Soros's 1992 sterling short; Druckenmiller's 1999 internet long-then-cover; Klarman's 2008-09 deployment; Marks's late-2007 "the race to the bottom" memo and 2008-09 distressed deployment.
- The student develops their own frame — explicit choices on the five axes above.

### Stage 4: Synthesis and edge

- Where the canon is contested: the post-2010 challenge to value investing; the platform/network-effect challenge to moat doctrine; the macro regime-change question post-2022.
- Cross-domain integration: information theory (Shannon), behavioral economics (Kahneman/Thaler), network theory, complexity theory.
- The student's own positions defended in debate mode against the canon.

## Live debates the student should engage

Questions on which serious people disagree right now and the student should be able to argue on either side:

1. **Has value investing been permanently impaired by the dominance of intangible-asset businesses?** The 2010-2020 underperformance is suggestive; the rebound 2022-2024 cuts the other way.
1. **Are platform moats durable or are they more like 1960s broadcast-network moats — feeling permanent right up until they aren't?** The case for permanence: network effects compound. The case against: regulation, AI disruption, fashion shifts.
1. **Is the post-2008 monetary regime fundamentally different and does it require different investing rules?** The bull case: no, cycles still apply. The bear case: ZIRP + QE + fiscal dominance has changed asset-price formation.
1. **Is private equity's reported performance survivorship-biased survivorship of survivorship?** The case for skepticism: Phalippou, Ilmanen. The case for the asset class: persistent outperformance in top-quartile funds, illiquidity premium.
1. **Is concentrated stock picking dead at scale or do dedicated operators still extract alpha?** The case for dead: Berkshire's recent underperformance, the rise of indexing, factor-mining. The case against: Renaissance, certain hedge funds, persistent outperformers exist.

## What this domain assumes

The student already understands:

- Basic accounting and financial statement structure.
- The vocabulary of public-market investing (P/E, EV/EBITDA, IRR, etc.).
- Enough math to read the academic literature.

If any of these are missing, do prerequisite work first. Quality investing teaching requires fluency in the basics; without it, the council can't be run productively.

## Note on cross-domain connection

When the student has a trading background, investing-domain teaching is most productive when bridged to trading-domain material (see `trading.md`) and when the cross-domain connections are made explicit — they share dynamics, diverge in time horizons, and the disciplines genuinely interact. Force the bridge; don't let the student treat them as separate intellectual worlds.

When the student has no trading background, do not force a trading-investing bridge. Build investing as its own discipline first.
