# Domain: Decision-Making

Judgment and choice under uncertainty. The discipline that underlies every other domain in this skill — investing, trading, strategy, business — because all of them are special cases of "decide well when you don't have full information."

## The council

### 1. Daniel Kahneman

**Position:** Human reasoning is systematically biased in identifiable ways. System 1 (fast, intuitive, heuristic) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical) operate together, with System 1 dominating most everyday cognition. Prospect theory: losses loom larger than gains, and choice is reference-dependent.

**Canonical sources:** *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). The original 1979 paper with Tversky on prospect theory. *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* (2021, with Sibony and Sunstein).

**Where to press him:** Many of the original heuristics-and-biases experiments have had mixed replication. Which findings are robust and which were artifacts? *Noise* is more recent and arguably more practically important than *Thinking, Fast and Slow* — but it's been less widely read. Why?

### 2. Amos Tversky

**Position:** Kahneman's intellectual partner. The mathematical formulator of prospect theory and the key experimental designer. Often the sharper edge of the duo.

**Canonical sources:** The Tversky-Kahneman papers from 1971-1996. *The Undoing Project* (Lewis, 2016) — a biography of the collaboration. *Preference, Belief, and Similarity* (collected papers, 2004).

**Where to press him:** Died in 1996, didn't see most of the replication crisis and the subsequent reassessment. What would he have said about the failed replications? About the rise of behavioral economics as a field?

### 3. Philip Tetlock

**Position:** Most political and geopolitical experts forecast worse than random. A small subset — "superforecasters" — do dramatically better, and their methods are learnable. Active updating, probabilistic thinking, avoidance of narrative-driven prediction, calibration via aggregation of considerations.

**Canonical sources:** *Expert Political Judgment* (2005). *Superforecasting* (2015, with Dan Gardner). The Good Judgment Project research.

**Where to press him:** The superforecaster framework has been highly influential and is partly methodologically self-confirming (selecting good forecasters then describing what good forecasters do). What's load-bearing about the methodology vs. what's selection? How well does it transfer to other domains beyond geopolitical forecasting?

### 4. Annie Duke

**Position:** Decision quality is separate from outcome quality. Good decisions can produce bad outcomes (and vice versa) because of variance. Resulting — judging a decision by its outcome — is the cardinal sin of decision-making in any high-variance domain. Probabilistic thinking and "betting on your beliefs" as practices.

**Canonical sources:** *Thinking in Bets* (2018). *How to Decide* (2020). *Quit* (2022, on knowing when to stop).

**Where to press her:** The decision-vs-outcome framing is correct and important. The practical implementation — how do you actually judge a decision absent the outcome? — is harder than the rhetoric suggests. What's her method beyond "ask whether you'd make the same decision again"?

### 5. Herbert Simon

**Position:** Bounded rationality. Real decision-makers satisfice rather than optimize because optimization is computationally infeasible in the real world. Heuristics are not failures of rationality but rational adaptations to limited cognition.

**Canonical sources:** *Administrative Behavior* (1947). *The Sciences of the Artificial* (1969). *Models of My Life* (autobiography, 1991). The Nobel Prize lecture.

**Where to press him:** Simon's framework is hugely influential and somewhat displaced in popular discourse by the Kahneman-Tversky tradition, which framed heuristics more negatively. The "ecological rationality" tradition (Gigerenzer) rebuilds Simon. Which view is correct, and in which contexts?

### 6. Gerd Gigerenzer

**Position:** Simple heuristics are often better than complex models, especially under uncertainty (vs. risk). "Fast and frugal" decision-making. The heuristics-and-biases tradition mischaracterizes everyday cognition as failing relative to optimal decision theory — but optimal decision theory often isn't even definable in real-world settings.

**Canonical sources:** *Simple Heuristics That Make Us Smart* (1999). *Gut Feelings* (2007). *Risk Savvy* (2014). Various papers on the take-the-best heuristic.

**Where to press him:** Gigerenzer's findings are strongest where the environment is reasonably stable; under regime change, simple heuristics adapted to the old regime fail. How do you distinguish "the heuristic is the right tool" from "the heuristic worked in the past regime"?

### 7. Richard Feynman (on epistemic discipline)

**Position:** "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool." Active hostility to self-deception. The willingness to say "I don't know." Rigorous empirical discipline.

**Canonical sources:** *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985). *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* (1999). The Cargo Cult Science commencement speech (Caltech, 1974). *The Character of Physical Law* (1965).

**Where to press him:** Feynman's epistemic discipline is hard to operationalize for non-physicists. The standards he applied in physics — predictive precision tested against experiment — are not available in most decision domains. How do you transfer the spirit when the structure isn't there?

### 8. Charlie Munger (here, on decision-making rather than investing)

**Position:** The latticework of mental models from multiple disciplines. The willingness to do nothing for long stretches. Active inversion — solve a problem by asking what would cause failure, then avoid those things. The recognition that being not stupid is more important than being smart.

**Canonical sources:** *Poor Charlie's Almanack*. The "Psychology of Human Misjudgment" speech (Harvard Law School, 1995, revised later).

**Where to press him:** The 25 biases in the Misjudgment speech overlap heavily with the Kahneman-Tversky framework — and Munger doesn't fully credit the academic source. What does Munger add beyond the academic literature? His own self-description as a "human Berkshire annual meeting" — is the framework actually how he thinks or a post-hoc story?

### 9. Robert Rubin

**Position:** "In an uncertain world, judgments are about probabilities, not certainties." Treasury Secretary decision-making framed explicitly probabilistically. Treating high-stakes decisions as probability-weighted bets, not as right/wrong choices.

**Canonical sources:** *In an Uncertain World* (2003).

**Where to press him:** Rubin's framework worked well in the 1990s and was challenged by the 2008 crisis, which Rubin himself was peripherally involved in via Citigroup. Did the framework fail, or did execution fail? How does probabilistic thinking handle situations where the underlying probability distribution is itself uncertain?

### 10. Nassim Taleb (here, on epistemics)

**Position:** Most of our knowledge is mid-range knowledge of a fat-tailed world. Apparent precision in our predictions is mostly illusory. The Black Swan — the high-impact, low-probability event we systematically fail to anticipate. Skin in the game as an epistemic discipline.

**Canonical sources:** *Fooled by Randomness* (2001). *The Black Swan* (2007). *Antifragile* (2012). *Skin in the Game* (2018).

**Where to press him:** The fat-tail/Black Swan framing is correct and has been somewhat hardened into a brand. When is the world actually fat-tailed and when is it mostly Gaussian? What does Taleb's framework recommend doing differently than the standard prudent practitioner already does?

## The major axes of disagreement

### Axis 1: How biased is everyday cognition?

- **Heavily biased, in systematic ways:** Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler, the standard behavioral framework
- **Adaptively rational in many environments:** Simon, Gigerenzer
- **Heavily biased but the right response is institutional design, not individual cognition:** Modern policy implications (libertarian paternalism)

### Axis 2: Optimization vs. satisficing

- **Try to optimize where possible:** Most of decision theory
- **Optimization is rarely achievable; satisfice with good heuristics:** Simon, Gigerenzer
- **Optimization can be locally rational and globally pathological (Goodhart's law):** Complexity-aware practitioners

### Axis 3: Probabilities vs. scenarios

- **Probabilistic thinking, calibrated estimates:** Tetlock, Rubin, Duke
- **Scenario thinking under fat-tailed uncertainty:** Taleb, scenario-planning tradition (Shell, Wack)
- **Both, depending on the distribution:** Most sophisticated practitioners

### Axis 4: Individual cognition vs. systems

- **Improve individual cognition:** Most of the rationality literature, Kahneman's later work pivoted toward this
- **Improve institutions and incentives:** Hayek, much of policy economics, principal-agent theory
- **Improve aggregation/markets:** The wisdom-of-crowds literature, prediction markets

### Axis 5: Skin in the game

- **Essential for epistemic discipline:** Taleb, much of practitioner culture
- **Not essential; theory can be sound without it:** Most of academic decision theory
- **Useful but not necessary; institutions can substitute:** Mixed views

## Curriculum sequence

### Stage 1: Orientation

- The Kahneman-Tversky framework: System 1/System 2, the major biases (availability, anchoring, representativeness, loss aversion), prospect theory.
- Probability basics: base rates, conditional probability, Bayes, expected value, variance.
- The decision-vs-outcome distinction (Duke).
- The Simon vs. K-T tension and Gigerenzer's reframing.

### Stage 2: Mechanism

- Why each of the major biases exists from an evolutionary/ecological standpoint. The trade-offs they're making.
- How superforecasters actually work (Tetlock): granular probability estimation, active updating, breaking problems into components, calibration tracking.
- Bayesian updating in practice. Where it's actually feasible and where it isn't.
- The mathematics of Kelly criterion, expected utility, and the discipline of probabilistic position-taking.

### Stage 3: Application

- Real decision cases the student works through: career decisions, investment decisions, organizational decisions, geopolitical predictions.
- Pre-mortems and post-mortems as decision tools.
- Decision journaling — the practice of recording the reasoning before the outcome to enable real learning later.
- Calibration practice: making forecasts with explicit probabilities and tracking calibration over time.

### Stage 4: Synthesis and edge

- The intersection with strategy, investing, organizational design.
- Where the rationality literature is itself biased — replication issues, generalization issues, the gap between WEIRD-population studies and human variation.
- The student's own decision-making profile: where they're systematically miscalibrated, where their judgment is reliable.
- Active practice of high-stakes decision-making with rigorous review.

## Live debates

1. **How much of the K-T canon has survived replication?** Some of the most famous findings have been weakened (priming effects, ego depletion). What's robust and what's not?
1. **Is the "rationality improvement" literature actually changing behavior, or is it mostly read by people who already operate this way?** The transfer problem from understanding biases to acting differently.
1. **Are prediction markets the future of forecasting, or do they have structural limits (manipulation, low participation in tails)?** The Polymarket-era empirical record.
1. **Does AI-augmented decision-making actually improve human judgment, or does it produce new biases (automation bias, anchoring on model output)?** Increasingly important question.
1. **Skin in the game vs. detachment — is operator-grade thinking always better than academic thinking, or only in some domains?**

## What this domain assumes

The student already understands:

- Basic probability and statistics.
- Willingness to log their own predictions and review them honestly.
- The practical context where decisions matter (their own life, work, investment, etc.).

This is the most universally applicable domain. It interacts directly with every other domain in this skill.
